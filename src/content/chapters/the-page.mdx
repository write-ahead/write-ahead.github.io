Everything a database keeps on disk is cut into fixed-size blocks called
<Annotate client:visible type="underline">pages</Annotate> — usually 8&nbsp;KB each. The
table isn't one long scroll of rows; it's a pile of these pages, and *page zero* is where
the file begins. So before anything else, it's worth knowing what's inside a single one.

The trick every engine uses is the **slotted page**. The page fills from *both ends*: a
small directory of slot pointers grows down from the top, the row data grows up from the
bottom, and the free space in the middle is whatever's left.

<Figure caption="Press insert — a slot appears up top, its row appears at the bottom, and the free space between them shrinks.">
  <SlottedPage client:visible />
</Figure>

Why split it like this? Because rows are different sizes and they come and go. If you only
ever appended rows, you'd have to shuffle everything whenever one in the middle was
deleted. Instead the <Annotate client:visible type="circle" token="--accent2">slot directory</Annotate>
is a layer of indirection: slot *2* always means "row 2," even if the engine later moves
that row's bytes around inside the page to reclaim space.

<MarginNote>
  <b>Why fixed-size pages at all?</b> Disks and the OS move data in fixed blocks, and the
  buffer pool caches whole pages in memory. A fixed size makes a page addressable by a
  single number — its <b>page id</b> — which is exactly what an index stores to point at a row.
</MarginNote>

## Reading a page, byte by byte

Peek at the first bytes of page zero and you'll see the header before any row data:

<div class="hexblock">
<span class="off">0000</span>  <span class="b0">00 00 00 00</span>  <span class="b0">0b 00</span>  <span class="b0">1f 20</span>   lsn · slots=11 · free=8223<br/>
<span class="off">0008</span>  <span class="b0">20 00</span> <span class="b0">00 20</span>        upper · lower pointers<br/>
<span class="off">000c</span>  <span class="b1">00 02 00 14</span> ...        slot 0 → (off 0x14, len 2)<br/>
<span class="off">....</span>  <span class="b2">00 00 00 00 00 00</span> ...   free space<br/>
<span class="off">1ff4</span>  <span class="b1">6b 61 6c 65 65 6d</span> ...   row bytes, growing upward
</div>

The <span class="ink-accent">header</span> is just bookkeeping: how many slots, where the
free space starts and ends. The <span class="ink-teal">committed rows</span> sit at the far
end. Everything in between is <span class="ink-red">free space</span> waiting to be claimed.

## What you now know

<ol class="steps">
  <li>A table is a pile of fixed-size <b>pages</b>, not one long list of rows.</li>
  <li>Inside a page, a <span class="ink-accent">slot directory</span> grows down and <span class="ink-teal">rows</span> grow up.</li>
  <li>Slots are a layer of indirection, so the engine can move row bytes without changing a row's identity.</li>
  <li>When the free space runs out, the page is <b>full</b> — and the row has to go somewhere else.</li>
</ol>

That last point is the whole reason indexes exist: once you have millions of pages, you
need a way to find the right one without reading them all. That's the next chapter.
